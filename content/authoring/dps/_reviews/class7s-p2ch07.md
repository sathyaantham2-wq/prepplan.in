(Findings are appended concept by concept; the severity-ordered summary is at the end of the file.)

## C7S-7.1 (20 read)
Keys all recomputed: no wrong keys. Case-study arithmetic verified (4.5x8=36; 36+24=60 only pair; 5400-1200=4200; 4200x2500=1.05 crore = 105 lakh).
- MEDIUM, Q7 (assertion_reason) "Physical infrastructure has four broad groups..." - the book says infrastructure "includes" those four "and so on", so "has four broad groups" claims more than the book (it is not presented as an exhaustive list). Fix: "Physical infrastructure includes transportation, utilities, communication and energy infrastructure." Same wording issue in Q9(a) "four groups ... listed" (acceptable) but Q7 is an outright assertion.
- MEDIUM, Q19 (case study, Chikhli panchayat) - part (c) "Which project is the least needed here, and why?" is answered by the stem ("their canals and electric pumps already work well"), a free mark (QUESTION_STANDARD s11). Part (b) is pure addition and needs no chapter. Fix: drop the sentence about canals working from the stem, and make (c) ask which project best protects Satish-type tomato growers' chain, using the book's chain (roads, cold storage, internet).
- LOW, Q20 (case study, hill village) - part (a) subtraction and (b) multiplication need no chapter; only (c)-(d) do. Acceptable only if (a) is merged; fix: make (a) "Using the chapter, name two infrastructure changes that explain the rise" is not needed; simplest is merge (a)+(b) into one part and add a chapter-based part (e.g. which group of infrastructure the road belongs to and which the homestay clinic belongs to).
- LOW, Q4/Q3/Q6 - wrong options lean on "only/alone/do not" while the key is the one unqualified option, so the cue gives the answer. Vary: make at least one distractor a plausible, non-absolute mis-reading (e.g. "Roads help farmers but social infrastructure replaces the need for markets").
- LOW, Q5 - distractors B, C (cold storage + canals; metro + solar) are unrelated to the emergency, easy elimination; Q1 fine. Q10 "four pieces" - canals and pumps are counted as one, ambiguity: say "name the four kinds of help".
- LOW, redundancy: the four-groups fact is tested in Q7, Q8, Q9 and part of Q11/Q14; Q9(a) and Q8 are the same recall. Replace Q9 with a different (a)/(b).
- LOW, Q13 - tagged Analyse but (a) is reading Fig. 7.3; and the book's own account of the journey (car parked at metro station, then train, then metro to railway station) is itself muddled; key "any two" is safe.
- Style: "Rs" used in Q19/Q20 instead of the rupee sign (QUESTION_STANDARD s5).
Verdict 7.1: keys sound; two case studies are partly free-mark arithmetic; some cueing.

## C7S-7.2 (20 read)
Keys recomputed: all correct (4,112/400 = 10.28 so 11 days, 112 km left; 4,112/150,000 = 2.74%; 6x4x450 = 10,800; x335 = 36,18,000; 9,00,000/7,000 = 128.6; 11,00,000/9,500 = 115.8; 440x5 = 2,200). Statement items: Q6 statements 1 and 3 false, 2 true, key "2 only" right; Q7 A false R true, right; Q8 I false (second longest), II true, right.
- MEDIUM, Q16 "Compare the Dhola Sadiya Bridge and a living root bridge on any three points: who or what builds it, the material and the time it takes." The book gives no builder, material or build time for Dhola Sadiya, so the key invents "built as a modern engineering structure" and cannot score a real comparison on those three points. Fix: compare on what it crosses/where, length or height (9.15 km vs 15-30 m high), why it matters (year-round travel vs cultural meaning), and how long it lasts.
- MEDIUM, Q19 (case study, Roshan's trucks) - (c) and (d) do not connect: the Rs 36.18 lakh saving is never used in the decision, and since it exceeds both purchases the line "lets him buy one item first" is unmotivated. The truck repays faster on the figures alone (116 vs 129 days), so the "reason from the chapter" is bolted on and the decision has no real trade-off. Also (a) is a pure recall of "four hours" (acceptable) but (b) and the payback are arithmetic only. Fix: make (d) ask whether the saving from (c) covers each purchase and compare payback with the flood-day loss, or tune the numbers so cold storage wins on 365-day spoilage (e.g. Rs 12,000 a day).
- LOW, Q20 (case study, Guwahati school bus) - (b) 440 x 5 is free arithmetic; (d) is an open "either trip" with no real trade-off. Realism: a school bus on a 440 km one-way trip five times a month is not credible; in reality Guwahati-Tezpur is a national highway, so labelling it a "state highway" is a factual slip for an Assam setting. The stem says "A table lists" but gives a list. Fix: use a smaller local trip pair, invent a district road name, and say "list".
- LOW, Q14 and Q15 (show-impossible, claim-check) - both claims are strawmen (NH44 longer than the whole network; ferry "as good as" a bridge), so the verdict is trivial. Make the claim subtler, e.g. "NH44 is about a quarter of the national highway network" (then the student must compute 2.7 per cent).
- LOW, Q8 labelled Analyse/Hard but is a two-fact true/false check; Q17 and Q18 (5 marks) are recall of one passage with one mark per recalled fact; label Understand/Remember or add a "why" part. Q12 duplicates content later rewarded in Q17.
- LOW, distractors: Q2 options B and D ("Srinagar and Kolkata in the east") are arbitrary, not chapter confusions; use Golden Quadrilateral city pairs (Delhi-Mumbai) only if clearly distinct from NH44. Q5 options C and D ("both") are weak; the stem tells which government keeps the first stretch.
- LOW, level mix: more than half of the concept is retrieval of the same two pages (Q1-3, 5-8, 10-13, 17, 18); only 14, 15, 16, 19, 20 reason. Convert two of Q10/Q12/Q13 to application.
Verdict 7.2: keys correct; a recall-heavy concept with one invented comparison basis and a case study decision that ignores its own data.

- LOW, Q5 distractors C ("air ... carries heavy goods cheaply") and B ("roads reach every power plant door") contain absolute or false-looking claims; fine, but D (metro, 23 cities) is not a real confusion for a coal job.

## C7S-7.4 (20 read)
Keys recomputed: all correct (3,760 lakh / 159 = 23.6; 12/229 = 5.2%; 12,000 x 33 = 3,96,000; prawn case 40,00,000 value, air 7,20,000 and 6,40,000, sea 14,80,000 and 5,20,000; 4,20,000 x 1.5 = 6,30,000 TEU, x33 = 2,07,90,000 m3 = 2.08 crore, shortfall 80,000 TEU = 26,40,000 m3). Q6 statement 3 false (TEU is capacity, not speed), key right.
- MEDIUM, Q17 (5 marks) final clause "which of the two links India more strongly to West Asia, Africa and Europe" - the book says only that the coastline "is connected to West Asia, Africa, and Europe, supporting trade through shipping", and also says air transport "is the fastest way to connect people and places within and across countries". It never ranks them, and the key's "in bulk" is the author's wording. A second defensible answer exists (air for people). - MEDIUM, Q8 Statement I "Ships are a cheaper mode than other modes for carrying heavy goods over long distances" overclaims: the book says ships are "a cheaper mode of transport" and says cargo trains also carry coal with 75-90% less energy than road; "than other modes" is not in the book and a student who knows rail is cheap can justify marking it false. Fix: "Ships are a cheaper mode of transport and carry heavy goods such as coal, cars and cement over long distances."
- LOW, Q20 (case study, Sagar Port) - (d) has no trade-off ("any reasoned way"); the problem equates cargo growth with container-capacity need, which the book does not say, and (a)-(c) are arithmetic after the single recalled 50%. - LOW, Q19 (prawns) is the best case study in the chapter (a genuine trade-off: air wins at 3%, sea at 1%), but (b), (c) need no chapter knowledge and (a) is partly given away by "chilled prawns" in the stem; accept, or let (a) read "which mode, according to the chapter, suits perishable cargo, and its cost".
- LOW, Q7 R (376 million passengers) is unrelated trivia to A (garden-city airport); s11 asks R to be a real fact about the topic and explanatory or tellingly not. Prefer an R about airports being designed for passengers' comfort or about green design (e.g. the Cochin solar project, Fig. 7.2).
- LOW, distractors: Q2 D ("Konkan coast") and C ("busiest container port") are invented, not chapter confusions; Q5 B ("159 airports") and D (metro) are throwaways; Q4 C, D are the same weak pair as 7.3 Q5. Q13 is two trivial lines; Q16 (clues for 'a port') is a reverse in name only (recall).
- LOW, redundancy: Q1, Q13, Q14 and Q17 repeat one fact (air high-value/perishable, ships heavy); Q9/Q10 are the same "state then compute" frame.
Verdict 7.4: keys correct, strongest case study (Q19) and a good show-impossible (Q15); two statements overclaim beyond the book.
