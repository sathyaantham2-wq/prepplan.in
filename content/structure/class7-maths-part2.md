## Part II Ch 1: Geometric Twins (source gegp201)
- C7M-1.1 Congruent figures. Same shape and size; rotating or flipping does not change a figure; a circle is fixed by its radius, a rectangle by its two sides.
- C7M-1.2 SSS condition and naming congruent triangles. Three equal sides give congruence; in triangle ABC congruent to XYZ, A matches X, B matches Y, C matches Z, and matching parts are equal.
- C7M-1.3 SAS condition, and why AAA and SSA fail. Two sides and the included angle; equal angles alone give the same shape but maybe different sizes; SSA can give two different triangles.
- C7M-1.4 ASA and AAS conditions. Two angles and the included side (ASA); two angles and a non-included side (AAS) after finding the third angle as 180 minus the other two.
- C7M-1.5 RHS condition and the list of conditions. Right triangles with equal hypotenuse and one equal side; the conditions are SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS.
- C7M-1.6 Angles of isosceles and equilateral triangles. Angles opposite equal sides are equal; equilateral angles are 60 each; isosceles base angles (180 - a)/2.
- C7M-1.7 Using congruence in figures. Find the equal parts (given, common side, vertically opposite or alternate angles), match a condition, write the congruence in order, then corresponding parts are equal.
IN: congruence meaning; which measurements recreate a figure; SSS; naming corresponding parts and writing the congruence in order; why AAA fails; SAS; why SSA fails; ASA and AAS; RHS and the list of five conditions; angles opposite equal sides, isosceles base angles, equilateral 60; using congruence with midpoints, vertically opposite and alternate angles, common sides, squares and rectangles.
OUT: similarity and scale factors (only used to show AAA fails); formal two-column proofs (the chapter argues in words); Pythagoras and hypotenuse calculation; congruence of quadrilaterals/polygons; area and perimeter of congruent figures.

## Part II Ch 2: Operations with Integers (source gegp202)
- C7M-2.1 Integers on the number line and the token model. Final position = first + second movement; a - b = a + (-b); the inverse of a is -a; from sum S and difference D the numbers are (S+D)/2 and (S-D)/2.
- C7M-2.2 Multiplication of integers with tokens. 4x2=8, 4x(-2)=-8, (-4)x2=-8, (-4)x(-2)=8.
- C7M-2.3 Patterns in integer multiplication and the sign rules. +x+ and -x- are positive, +x- is negative; commutative; (-1) x a = -a.
- C7M-2.4 Division of integers. a/(-b) = -(a/b), (-a)/b = -(a/b), (-a)/(-b) = a/b.
- C7M-2.5 Word problems with integers. Total = (gains x gain) + (losses x (-loss)); position = start + time x signed speed.
- C7M-2.6 Associativity and the sign of many products. Positive with an even number of negative factors, negative with an odd number.
- C7M-2.7 Distributive property and expressions with integers. a(b+c) = ab + ac for all integers; the inverse of a sum is the sum of the inverses.
IN: sum and difference to find two numbers; coin-strike model P = a + b; number-line movements; green/red tokens and zero pairs; multiplication with tokens; patterns in multiplication tables; Brahmagupta's fortune and debt rules; word problems (negative marking, lift up and down, speed and direction); magic integer grid; division as a missing factor; temperature falls, profit/loss per item; associativity and many-factor signs; distributive property incl. tokens and pattern machines; multi-step expressions and puzzles.
OUT: negative fractions/decimals (rational numbers); exponents with negative bases; order-of-operations rules beyond brackets, terms and multiplication; solving equations with integer coefficients (Part II Ch 7); division by zero; formal proof of the distributive law.

## Part II Ch 3: Finding Common Ground (source gegp203)
- C7M-3.1 HCF by listing common factors. The HCF (GCD) is the highest common factor; the longest jump from 0 landing on both numbers.
- C7M-3.2 Prime factorisation and factors from it. Divide repeatedly by primes until the quotient is 1; a product of some of the primes is a factor; one counterexample disproves a claim (conjecture / counterexample).
- C7M-3.3 HCF using prime factorisation. Each common prime the minimum number of times; no common prime means HCF 1.
- C7M-3.4 LCM by listing multiples and by prime factorisation. Each prime the maximum number of times; common multiples never end.
- C7M-3.5 Patterns, properties and generalisations about HCF and LCM. One number a factor of the other: HCF is the smaller; consecutive numbers HCF 1; consecutive even HCF 2; doubling both doubles the HCF; HCF of multiples of k is k x HCF of the multipliers.
- C7M-3.6 Division method and HCF x LCM = product. HCF = product of divisors; LCM = HCF x remaining numbers of the last row; HCF x LCM = product of the two numbers, so LCM is never more than the product.
- C7M-3.7 Word problems with HCF and LCM. Largest equal pieces/cube: HCF; first common time/smallest common length: LCM; numbers with given remainders: LCM plus the remainder.
IN: common factors and HCF by listing (largest square tile, fewest equal bags); longest jump; prime factorisation by division; listing factors from prime factorisation; counterexamples; HCF by primes; LCM by listing (shortest toran, next free gajak) and by primes; special-pair generalisations (factor, consecutive, even/odd, co-prime, doubled, common multipliers); division method for HCF and LCM; HCF x LCM = product; word problems (cowherd, packing cubes, smallest number with given divisors).
OUT: Euclid's algorithm; adding unlike fractions through the LCM as a topic; divisibility tests beyond Class 6 and modular arithmetic proofs; proof of HCF x LCM = product for three or more numbers.

## Part II Ch 4: Another Peek Beyond the Point (source gegp204)
- C7M-4.1 Decimals as fractions, and multiplying or dividing by 10, 100, 1000. Dividing by 10/100/1000 moves the point left by the number of zeroes (add zeroes in front if needed); multiplying moves it right.
- C7M-4.2 Multiplying decimals. Remove the points, multiply as counting numbers, give the product as many decimal places as the two numbers have together.
- C7M-4.3 Size of a product and using a known product. Both > 1: larger than both; both between 0 and 1: smaller than both; one each: between; related products differ only in the point.
- C7M-4.4 Dividing decimals by counting numbers. Regroup the remainder into the next place, put the point in the quotient moving from ones to tenths, stop at remainder 0.
- C7M-4.5 Dividing by a decimal. Multiply divisor and dividend by 10, 100... until the divisor is a counting number; dividing by a number below 1 gives a quotient greater than the dividend.
- C7M-4.6 Decimal quotients that never end. Dividing by n leaves fewer than n different non-zero remainders, so the division ends or a remainder repeats and the digits cycle.
- C7M-4.7 Leap years and the calendar. Leap if divisible by 4, except century years which need divisibility by 400.
IN: decimals as fractions; multiply a decimal by a counting number and by a decimal via fractions; place the point by total decimal places; product bigger/smaller; known product to related products; word problems; divide by 10/100/1000; long division with regrouping incl. decimal dividends; divide by a decimal; non-terminating quotients (10 / 3, 1 / 7) and why remainders repeat; leap year rules and counting days over 100 and 1000 years.
OUT: bar notation for recurring decimals and converting back to fractions; rounding/estimating decimals to places; cyclic numbers like 142857 and Artin's conjecture; Hidato puzzle.

## Part II Ch 5: Connecting the Dots (source gegp205)
- C7M-5.1 Statistical questions and statements. A statistical question expects varied answers and is answered with data; a statistical statement summarises data with numbers, proportions or predictions.
- C7M-5.2 Arithmetic mean (average). Mean = sum of values / number of values; a fair share.
- C7M-5.3 Comparing data with range, totals and dot plots. Range = max - min; a dot plot shows spread and clusters but loses the original order.
- C7M-5.4 Median and outliers. Sort; the middle value, or the average of the two middle values for an even count; an outlier is far from the rest.
- C7M-5.5 Mean and median compared, zero and missing values. No outlier: close; high outlier: mean > median; low outlier: mean < median; a missing value is not counted, a zero is.
- C7M-5.6 Double bar (clustered column) graphs. Bar length = value / scale unit; colours or patterns for the two groups; identify what is given, then infer.
- C7M-5.7 Reading data tables and finding stories in data. Compare by subtracting; a statement is justified only if the numbers show it; one or two samples cannot be generalised.
IN: statistical questions and statements; mean as fair share and comparing groups of different sizes; min, max, range, total, dot plots; median with odd/even counts, outliers and their effect on the mean; mean vs median and which represents the data; read and draw double bar graphs, choose a scale; read tables and graphs, decide which statements the data justify.
OUT: mode and frequency tables; pie charts and line graphs; standard deviation; number-lock puzzle and projects.

## Part II Ch 6: Constructions and Tilings (source gegp206)
- C7M-6.1 Perpendicular bisector and points equidistant from two points. Any point equidistant from X and Y lies on the perpendicular bisector of XY, and it is the only such line.
- C7M-6.2 Constructing the perpendicular bisector and a 90 degree angle. Same radius from X and Y for each pair of arcs; for 90 degrees at O first mark X and Y at equal distances so O is the midpoint.
- C7M-6.3 Bisecting and copying angles. Bisect: OA = OB, equal arcs from A and B meet at C, OC bisects; copy an angle by copying an isosceles triangle by SSS.
- C7M-6.4 Constructing parallel and perpendicular lines. Copy the corresponding angle at the new point; for a perpendicular from P cut the line at X and Y with PX = PY and bisect XY.
- C7M-6.5 Angles at a point, 60 degrees and the regular hexagon. Angles around a point total 360; six equilateral triangles fill it; regular hexagon angles are 120 and its centre is a side-length from every vertex.
- C7M-6.6 Tiling regions with 2 x 1 tiles. An odd number of squares cannot be tiled; a tileable region has equal black and white squares; a grid with one even side can be tiled.
- C7M-6.7 Tiling the plane. Squares (4 x 90), equilateral triangles (6 x 60), regular hexagons (3 x 120) tile the plane; combinations tile if angles add to 360.
IN: perpendicular bisector and the congruence justification; construct it and a 90 degree angle (and the Sulba-Sutra rope method); bisect and copy angles, 45 degrees and eight-petalled designs; parallel line by copying a corresponding angle and a perpendicular from an outside point; 60, 120, 30, 15 degrees, regular hexagon and six-pointed star; tileability by counting and colouring; tiling the plane with squares, triangles, hexagons.
OUT: regular pentagon; constructing an exact measure like 65.5 degrees; tangram and optical illusions; general proof of which polygons tile.
